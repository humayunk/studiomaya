Two sides of a patient summary.

01 · What I heard

Feel heard first, then find what helps.

You're rebuilding Recovapro around helping people feel heard, understand their pain in context and find what helps, with the patient summary as one part of that. Here's what I took from your note and your site:

  • The people who showed up were in pain. At your retail sites, roughly seven in ten visitors weren't athletes. That's the audience you're now building for.
  • RecovaSmart starts with the person's story. Where it hurts, what it feels like and what it stops them doing, in their own words. Then a body map, pain alongside daily life and what they've already tried, and possibilities that come with their reasons and what's still uncertain.
  • The care team leads. Your new pain guides, including the cancer pain ones, make no promises and send people back to their care team with better questions. That restraint is right, and the summary should carry it.

So the summary has two jobs. It has to make the person feel heard, and it has to be useful to whoever treats them. I've designed for each side.

02 · The patient side

All My Parts: a summary people bring to their next session.

An AI journal for people in Internal Family Systems therapy. I designed, built and shipped it on my own. It's live on the web and the App Store, with paying users.

People log what comes up between therapy sessions, by voice or text. Each week an AI read pulls their entries together: what was most active, what set it off, what went well. The aim is not to replace the therapist. It's to give the person a clearer hand-off into their next session.

What testing taught me

  • The language mattered more than the AI. Describing someone's inner experience without it sounding clinical took several rewrites. The weekly summary talks to the person, not about them.
  • People wanted to talk, not type. In the web beta, voice entries worked and typing on a phone was the friction, along with the effort of opening an app to write at all. That's why I built the native iOS app.
  • The people who stayed were already in therapy. They used it between sessions. So the summary's real job is a hand-off, something you bring to the person treating you, not an end in itself.

03 · The clinician side

Clinical workflows: a summary a clinician can check.

Practice with Joy helps nurse practitioners run their own clinics on Canvas Medical, an EHR you build on top of. I designed the layer that takes the paperwork off them. Several clinics now run on it.

Intake builds the patient's chart before anyone on staff touches it. During the visit, an ambient scribe turns the conversation into structured parts of the note, and the clinician approves each one. By the time the patient leaves, the note is done.

What I learned from the clinicians using it

  • They won't trust a summary they can't check. The scribe proposes, item by item, and the clinician approves. A block of generated text gets reread from scratch, which saves no time.
  • Show the trail. Intake shows exactly what it filled in and where it came from, so the clinician can trust what was assembled without redoing it.
  • Clinicians don't want more software. The changes that landed were the ones that removed a step entirely. The question was always how much a clinician has to trust before they'll let a step disappear.

04 · What it means for RecovaSmart

One story, two readers.

Put together, this is how I'd think about the patient summary:

  1. Keep their words, add structure underneath. The person sees their story told back in their own language. The clinician sees the same story organised to scan in a short appointment, with every line traceable to what the person actually said.
  2. Let people talk. Describing pain is closer to telling a story than filling in a form. In my beta, voice worked where typing didn't, and talking asks less of someone who is hurting.
  3. Show what's uncertain. Your "relevant possibilities, and what remains uncertain" is a problem I've worked on before. At Reputable Health I designed an AI that recommends how to run a clinical study, and the only way scientists trusted it was when every suggestion explained itself and showed its sources.
  4. Test with both readers, early. Put a clickable summary in front of a handful of people in pain and two or three clinicians before building the full app. The two groups will want different things from the same page, and it's cheaper to find that out on a prototype.

Full write-ups: All My Parts, Clinical workflows and Reputable Health.

05 · Open questions

What I'd want to know.

  • Who reads the summary first? The person in pain, their GP or physio, or a specialist team like the palliative teams your cancer guides point to? Each one needs a different summary.
  • How does it reach them? Shown on a phone in the appointment, printed, or sent ahead as a link. The answer changes the format a lot.
  • Which pain first? Everyday back and neck pain, the people who already buy your devices, or cancer-related pain. Different clinicians, different stakes.
  • What have you already tested? If people have seen the RecovaSmart concepts, I'd love to hear what they said.
